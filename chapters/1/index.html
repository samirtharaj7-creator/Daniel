Study Notes

Daniel 1:1

Daniel begins with a wound. Jerusalem, the city of the temple, is under siege; Jehoiakim, king of Judah, is weak; Nebuchadnezzar, king of Babylon, appears to hold the future. The opening line is historical, but it is not bare history. It places two kings, two kingdoms, and one covenant city before us, and it lets the reader feel the shock: the people who worship the true God have been defeated. The book does not begin with Daniel's courage; it begins with Judah's failure and God's people under discipline.

The date points to the first Babylonian pressure on Jerusalem, commonly placed in 605 B.C. There is a well-known comparison with Jeremiah's fourth-year wording, but the difference is best explained by different systems for counting royal years. Daniel writes from the Babylonian world, where accession-year reckoning was common; Jeremiah speaks from Judah's side of the same crisis. Rather than creating a contradiction, the two reckonings let us hear the exile from inside Babylon.

This opening setting matters for the whole book. Daniel's public witness begins with the first deportation and will stretch across the Babylonian period to the early Persian restoration horizon. By the time Babylon falls in 539 B.C., Daniel will have lived through roughly two-thirds of a century of exile, court service, prophecy, and faithful witness.

From the first line, the book raises its great question: who really controls history? From below, Nebuchadnezzar came and besieged. From above, the next verse will say the Lord gave. Daniel does not deny armies, politics, or imperial ambition, but he refuses to interpret them as ultimate. Babylon is powerful, but Babylon is not sovereign.

Symbols

Jerusalem and Babylon

Jerusalem represents covenant worship and the people called by God's name; Babylon represents empire, pride, false worship, and the pressure to reinterpret defeat without God.
